Foundation · Lesson 1

Defining hazardous materials

Recognize what makes a material hazardous when it is not properly controlled.

Core definition

In the supplied course material, a hazardous material is any substance or material that poses an unreasonable risk to health, safety, property, or the environment if it is not properly controlled during handling, storage, manufacture, processing, use, disposal, or transportation.

Key idea: Chemical, biological, and radiological products may be safely manufactured, used, transported, or stored every day. They become a concern when uncontrolled conditions create unreasonable risk.

Foundation · Lesson 2

How hazardous materials can harm you

Use TRACEM-P to remember seven mechanisms of harm.

TRACEM-P

The acronym represents potential hazards at an incident scene:

  • Thermal — heat or cold, including fire, chemical reaction, cryogenic liquids, or liquefied gases.
  • Radiological — exposure to materials that emit ionizing radiation.
  • Asphyxiating — oxygen displacement or toxic effects that prevent the proper use of oxygen.
  • Chemical — poisoning, respiratory injury, or corrosive damage.
  • Etiological/Biological — harm from pathogens, bacteria, viruses, or poisonous organisms.
  • Mechanical — debris, trips and falls, noise, and other physical hazards.
  • Psychological — mental harm caused by fear or witnessing a violent event.

Foundation · Lesson 3

Roles and responsibilities

Understand your role, your limitations, and when you cannot proceed further.

Awareness-level priorities

The material emphasizes a slow, methodical approach. First responders must understand the role they play, understand their limitations, and realize when they cannot proceed any further.

  • Recognize the presence of hazardous materials.
  • Protect yourself and avoid becoming exposed or contaminated.
  • Call for trained resources and communicate available information.
  • Support isolation and deny entry within your level of training and local procedures.

Recognition · Lesson 4

Seven clues to the presence of hazmat

Recognition information generally moves from lower-risk, distance-based clues toward clues that may require closer proximity or specialized equipment.

The seven clues

  1. Locations, occupancy types, and preincident surveys
  2. Basic container information
  3. Transportation placards, markings, and labels
  4. Nontransportation hazmat markings and labels
  5. Hazard information reference sources
  6. Sensory clues, including victim signs and symptoms
  7. Monitoring and detection devices
Distance often equates to safety. The closer you must be to identify a material, the greater the chance of exposure to harmful effects.

Initial information · Lesson 6

Hazard information resources

Use the right information source for fixed-facility and transportation incidents.

Common resources

  • Fixed facilities: safety data sheets, inventory records, facility documents, signs, markings, container shapes, and labels.
  • Transportation incidents: the Emergency Response Guidebook, shipping papers, placards, and container shapes.
  • Shipping-paper sequence: identification number, proper shipping name, hazard class or division, and packing group—ISHP.
  • SDS: a detailed 16-section information sheet supplied by a chemical manufacturer or importer.

Initial information · Lesson 7

Using the Emergency Response Guidebook

The ERG helps emergency responders identify initial hazards and protective actions during transportation emergencies.

Ways to locate an initial action guide

  • Use a four-digit UN identification number in the yellow-bordered pages.
  • Use the material name in the blue-bordered pages when the exact spelling is known.
  • Use a transportation placard and the placard reference table.
  • Use container profiles in the white pages to cross-reference container shapes and guide numbers.
Limitation: The supplied material states that the ERG is primarily designed for highways, railroads, and pipelines. It does not cover every fixed-facility or urban circumstance.

Protective actions · Lesson 8

Isolate and deny entry

Scene control protects personnel and the public by separating them from the source of harm and limiting cross-contamination.

Isolation

Isolation involves securing and maintaining the emergency scene by establishing perimeters or cordons and denying entry to unauthorized personnel. It also includes preventing contaminated people or animals from leaving the scene and spreading hazardous materials.

  • Establish an outer perimeter or outer cordon to control access and egress.
  • Use personnel at entrances for indoor incidents when appropriate.
  • Position safely and follow local policies, procedures, and the incident command structure.

Scope review · Lesson 9

Monitoring and detection devices

Understand why use of these devices is outside the awareness-level scope described in the supplied material.

Purpose and limitation

Monitoring and detection devices can help determine the presence, concentration, and scope of hazardous materials. However, the supplied course states that awareness-level personnel are not trained to use them. The devices require actual contact with the hazardous material to measure it, which is outside awareness-level actions.

Scope boundary: Recognize that monitoring may be needed, but do not perform work that exceeds your training and authorization.
